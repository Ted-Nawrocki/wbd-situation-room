Four days ago this was a two-week restraining order. Today it is a standstill that can run into the middle of next year.

## Just the facts

- Paramount filed an agreement on July 24, 2026 stating it will not take steps to close the acquisition until **June 1, 2027**, or **five days after** the state attorneys general and Writers Guild of America lawsuits are resolved, whichever comes first
- The August 3, 2026 preliminary injunction hearing has been cancelled by agreement of the parties
- The existing briefing schedule has been scrapped; the parties will file a joint statement on trial scheduling by July 31, 2026
- Paramount called the agreement a direct path to a trial based on the evidence; California AG Rob Bonta called it a significant win
- Shares of both Paramount and WBD fell on the news
- The merger agreement's outside date is March 4, 2027, with a one-time automatic extension to **June 4, 2027** if all conditions other than regulatory approvals are satisfied or waived
- The ticking fee, $0.25 per share per quarter, begins accruing September 30, 2026 regardless of the standstill, and could add roughly $1.7 billion to the deal price if the delay runs to June 2027
- **Correction:** the reverse termination fee payable by Paramount if the deal fails for regulatory reasons is **$7 billion**, not the $5.8 billion figure carried on this site in earlier updates. The $5.8 billion figure belonged to the earlier Netflix merger agreement's own reverse termination fee structure. This has been corrected throughout the site.

## Ted's Takes

A voluntary standstill running to June 2027 is a different animal than a court-imposed TRO. Nobody forced Paramount into this. Paramount chose a longer runway toward a full trial over continuing to fight an emergency motion on a compressed clock. That is a real strategic choice, and it says something about how Paramount is reading its odds: better served by a complete record than a rushed hearing.

For the corporate actions side, the operative fact is simple. The tender and exchange offers settle on the acquisition closing. The acquisition cannot close during the standstill. Every position tendered into those offers now sits in an unsettled state that could last the better part of a year, not weeks. That is a materially different operational posture than what this site described even a few days ago, and it is worth updating any internal tracking that assumed a Q3 or Q4 2026 resolution.

On the fee correction: getting the reverse termination fee right matters more here than in most contexts, because it is the number that explains Paramount's incentive to litigate rather than walk away. At $7 billion against roughly $650 million per quarter in ticking fees, Paramount would need close to eleven quarters of accruing ticking fees before the cost of delay caught up to the cost of losing outright. That math favors fighting this out, not settling early, and the standstill agreement is consistent with that read.
